    Context

    The half of a commerce platform that lives inside the shop.

    RA8M sells to businesses in Iraq. RA8M Store handles online selling for Instagram sellers and web shops. RA8M POS handles the daily work of a physical shop: products, billing, inventory, orders, purchasing and sales tracking. The constraint that shaped everything was range. A restaurant, a pharmacy and an electronics store all had to fit, in Arabic and English and in dinar, without becoming three separate products.

    The problem

    Fast for the cashier, trustworthy for the owner.

    The cashier at a busy counter needs a sale done in a few taps. The owner needs to know what is in stock, what customers owe and what sold, without opening three tools. And every kind of shop sells differently. If a POS suits one of them, each new customer means a rebuild. If it suits all of them loosely, it suits none of them well.

    Sells by count and by weight. Needs photos, fast taps and a note on the order.

    The call

    One shell. Four shops.

    I could have designed a separate POS for each kind of shop, or one generic POS with a pile of settings. I chose a third route: one shell that never changes, with the few parts that depend on the shop built to swap. The header, the rail and the order panel stay put. What changes is how products are shown, how quantity is entered and which back office modules appear.

    The cost: every new feature had to be designed for each shop type before it counted as done, which is a big part of why the file runs past 250 screens and states.

    The second call: selling and running the shop are two screens. A cashier in a queue never sees a general ledger.

    The design

    The order panel does three jobs, and the catalogue never leaves the screen.

    The order panel starts empty, fills as items are added, then turns into customer and payment details when the cashier taps Continue. The catalogue stays on screen the whole time, so adding a forgotten item is one tap, not a trip backwards. Customer details are optional: a walk-in name by default, a phone number if you have one, a new customer in a popup if you need one.

    Quantity the way each shop sells

    One plus button, three jobs. The shop type decides which one opens.

    Counted items

    The stepper sits right on the card. No popup for a second pizza.

    Loose goods

    A numpad with quarter, half and three-quarter shortcuts, because that is how a butcher counter talks.

    Pharmacy

    Batch number, expiry and stock on hand, so the cashier picks the batch instead of guessing.

    Customers and delivery

    A customer is a phone number first.

    In Iraq the phone number is how a shop knows a regular, so that is the field the cashier types into. Matches appear as you type. If there is no match, a popup opens with the number already filled in and asks only for a name, a city and an area. Once picked, the customer shows as a card with their address, and a clock on the order note sets a delivery day and time.

    The back office

    Grouped the way an owner thinks.

    The Admin Panel has its own sidebar: Analytics, Inventory, Sales, Purchases, Finance and Reports, then users and settings. Stock corrections happen in one table: the owner types what is actually on the shelf and the screen works out the difference. Reports reach all the way to a balance sheet, a general ledger and who owes what.

    Purchasing, payments and the ledger

    Stock comes in with a batch and leaves with one.

    A bill from a vendor is where batches are born: each item row opens into batch number, quantity and expiry. That is what makes the batch picker at the till possible. On the other side, a payment from a customer can be spread across several open invoices, part paid is a normal state, and the closing balance per customer rolls up into the general ledger.

    Items, raw materials and stock

    Everything the till sells starts here.

    An item is either ready stock or something the shop makes. Ready stock gets a quantity, a price and a unit. A cafe adds raw materials, flour and butter with their own units, and the stock page tracks what is low, what moved recently and what is about to expire, with every item expandable to its batches. Items can also be imported in bulk rather than typed one by one.

    Every screen size

    The same till in a pocket, and a front door for the product.

    On the phone the order panel becomes a bottom sheet, and the admin basics come along: items and filters, adding an item, invoices and a dashboard. The tablet keeps the desktop shell at a smaller size. The landing page presents three plans and four industries, with sign-up that verifies by OTP and a currency picker.

    What a cashier notices on day two.

    None of these is a feature anyone asks for in a sales call. All of them decide whether the till is trusted after a week on a real counter with a flaky connection, a scanner and three people sharing one login.

    The Cashier Log records who created, repriced, edited or deleted an order or invoice, and when. Owners asked for trust; this is what trust looks like.

    Discount the way the shop talks

    An amount or a percentage, one toggle. The totals block shows the discount as its own line so the customer sees the same thing the cashier typed.

    Iterations

    The design changed in the open.

    Options sit side by side in the file so the trade-off is visible, and two dated rounds changed how the product thinks about items. First units of measure, so a cart line can say 3 Pcs instead of just 3. Then variants, so a serum in 10 ml and 100 ml is one item with two rows, not two items.

    Three order rows, one frame

    Labelled, mid-density and compact versions of the Sales Register row, compared for how much each shows at a glance.

    Units of measure

    Items carry a unit, so the cart, the invoice and the stock table all speak the same language. Create Bill was reworked in the same round.

    Variants

    Name an attribute, list its values, get a variants table. SKUs and barcodes generate per row and a price set once copies to all.

    Working with AI

    The analytics page was written as HTML with Claude and brought into Figma as layers.

    The dashboard has the most repeated parts in the file: KPI tiles, charts and tables. So I decided what it should show, had Claude write it as markup on the RA8M tokens, and imported it into Figma as editable layers.

    1. Me

      Decide what the owner sees

      Which metrics exist, how they group into finance, sales, performance, inventory and purchases, and what sits at the top.

    2. Claude

      Build the page as one HTML file

      Every KPI tile, bar chart and table written as markup on the RA8M tokens, in one self-contained file.

    3. Figma

      Analyse and convert in Figma

      The HTML to Design plugin turns the page into proper, editable Figma layers.

    4. Me

      Adjust it like any other frame

      Review, regroup, restyle. Every call about hierarchy stayed mine; the typing did not.

    Where it went wrong: JavaScript charts vanished on import

    Charts that draw themselves with JavaScript do not survive the import. I asked for charts built from plain CSS bars and SVG paths instead, and they came through as editable shapes.

    Where it went wrong: the plugin connection kept failing

    The direct connection showed as connected but never ran. Pasting the HTML into the plugin by hand has been the reliable route, so that is the step I kept.

The file is organised by plan, so each tier on the landing page has its own page of screens behind it, and every feature exists for every shop type before it counts as done.

    What I would change

    Three things I would do next.

    Shop type as a component property

    Treat shop type as a property instead of copying frames. The Restaurant flow exists twice in the file today; one source would keep every shop type in step as features land.

    A stopwatch at a busy counter

    Put the weight pad and the batch picker in front of cashiers during a busy hour. They were shaped by how shops sell, and a stopwatch is the real test.

    AI for sample data, earlier

    Bring AI in earlier to generate realistic sample data per shop type, so a pharmacy mock never shows a pizza.
