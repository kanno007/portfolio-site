    Context

    Three surfaces, one record, built with a practising clinician.

    AesthetiDocs was founded by Dr Bonny Armstrong, a medical doctor who ran her own aesthetic clinic and built the first version of the product for it. Her clinical experience still shapes the consent, questionnaire and protocol templates. It is built for aesthetic clinics offering injectables, skin, laser and cosmetic treatments, and for the training academies that teach them.

    That focus matters more than it sounds. The product was not adapted from dental or general healthcare software, so the record looks different, and a lot of the decisions in this case study only make sense once you accept that an aesthetic clinic is a medical setting that also has to sell.

    I joined in November 2023 as Lead Designer and stayed until October 2026, leading a team of designers. I set the direction and the rules, split the modules across the team, reviewed their screens before anything went to the developers, and took the trade-offs to the founders in business terms. The brief, the research, the design system and the handoff ran through me. The screens were the team's work as much as mine, and the decisions below were the ones I made for them.

    The constraint that shaped everything

    Medicolegal compliance was the foundation rather than a feature. A questionnaire has to be complete, red flags reviewed, consent signed on the patient's device and filed against the treatment record, and emergency protocols one click away. Patient data sits in secure cloud storage with role-based access, audit logs and GDPR-compliant handling.

    The design consequence is simple to say and hard to do: the safe way has to be the easy way. Any form that can be skipped will be skipped on a busy afternoon, so every flow had to make the compliant path the shortest one.

    The problem in business terms

    Three disconnected experiences, one patient, many hidden problems.

    An aesthetic clinic looks simple on the outside, but behind the scenes it is a mix of bookings, consent, treatment notes and stock. Because these lived in different places, the same patient story was fragmented, increasing risk, creating extra work and leaving revenue on the table.

    Front of house

    A fragmented booking and check-in experience

    Bookings arrived by phone, by message and through a web form, and someone merged them into one diary by hand. The deposit was a separate conversation on a card machine that knew nothing about the diary. Front of house hated asking a new patient for money, so often they did not. And the no-shows clustered exactly there: new patients who had paid nothing were the ones who did not turn up.

    I hate asking new patients for a deposit at the desk.
    Practitioner

    Clinical and consent records spread across places

    Consent was signed on paper between appointments. Notes were written after the last patient had left. The questionnaire, the consent form and the treatment record lived in three places, so a missing form was discovered after the treatment rather than before it. In a medicolegal setting that is the practitioner's registration on the line.

    My notes get written after hours. Consent is on paper somewhere.
    Operations (stock and team)

    Stock, rooms and CRM were hard to track

    Stock sat in a spreadsheet that the diary and the card machine never saw, so products ran out unnoticed. Rotas, rooms and machines could be double-booked. Leads from the website, social media and the phone landed in different inboxes with nobody owning them. Revenue, rebooking and outstanding balances needed a manual tally, so the honest answer to how the clinic was doing was a guess from the bank balance.

    One platform

    Bring bookings, consent, treatment notes, payments, stock and team management into a single, connected experience with the patient at the centre.

    The growth challenge

    Every new clinic was set up manually: services, team, templates and the booking page. The product needed to give clinics a connected, compliant and easy-to-run experience from day one, and manual setup made it hard to onboard quickly and nearly impossible to scale cost-effectively.

    Goal, from the top of the research board: create a sign-up that encourages clinics to sign themselves up, set up the clinic and play around with the product before anyone from AesthetiDocs gets involved.

    What that meant for the brief

    Four sentences on the homepage carry the whole answer, and each one is a commitment I had to make real.

    One place for bookings, records and payments.

    Consent and forms completed before the needle goes anywhere near the patient.

    Stock, staff and rooms visible at a glance.

    A booking website that sells for the clinic 24 hours a day.

    The call

    Five decisions, each with what it cost.

    These were mine to make as the lead. I set the direction, the team worked the screens with me, I took the trade-offs to the founders in business terms, and I signed off what the developers built against the file.

    Self sign-up, but in layers

    What I chose

    Self-serve, split into three layers so nobody is asked for everything at once. Sign-up takes the minimum to create an account. Onboarding takes what makes the clinic recognisable. Everything else moves inside the product and is guided from there, up to a first appointment.

    On the table

    Keep white-glove setup, where AesthetiDocs staff configure every clinic (high quality, zero scale), or let clinics sign themselves up, knowing that a blank product is where most trials die.

    Why

    The moment an owner decides whether to stay is the first booking landing in their own diary, not a complete profile. Anything not needed for that is deferred and nudged. A clinic that gives up halfway still has a usable account rather than a half-filled form.

    What it cost

    Every empty state and first-run moment became design work, because the product now does the hand-holding a person used to. By version two, sign-up had grown to include plan selection, trial terms, optional card capture and a setup checklist.

    A booking flow that handles the edge cases

    What I chose

    Five steps, with completed steps stacked above the active one so the patient always sees what they have chosen and can change it without starting over. Inside the steps sit the decisions that make it work: next available practitioner as an explicit option, an information pop-up on treatments that need a prior consultation, practitioner profiles with experience and training, a waitlist when a day is full, deposit or full payment at checkout, a five-minute hold, and an honest error when someone else took the slot.

    On the table

    The simplest booking website is pick a treatment, pick a slot, pay. It is also wrong for this market. Real clinics run more than one location, practitioners are fully booked for weeks, some treatments need a consultation first, most need a deposit, and the best patients book again and again.

    Why

    Every one of those decisions removes a phone call to the clinic. The waitlist turns a lost booking into a lead with dates attached. The consultation pop-up stops a patient booking a top-up they are not eligible for.

    What it cost

    Far more states to design and test: 129 desktop and 133 mobile screens outside the archive, designed side by side so the two never drifted, and three major versions before the flow settled.

    On the table

    Clinics kept telling the same story: no-shows clustered on first appointments, and front of house hated asking for money. The cheap fix is a reminder email. The real fix is structural.

    Why

    A rule the product follows for you is worth more than a reminder a human has to remember. A patient who has paid something turns up, and reception no longer opens the relationship with an awkward request.

    What it cost

    Stripe had to be in the booking flow from day one, and the review step carries money maths that most booking forms never show, which made it the most revised screen in the file.

    Leads and tasks inside the record, not a bolt-on CRM

    What I chose

    Both live inside the app and link to the same people. A lead carries name, phone, email, created date, source, status, owner and the latest note, with the list split into new, open and done. From a lead the team can add notes, create a task from a note, change status, create the appointment with the deposit shown, add the person as a patient, or read the history. Waitlist requests arrive here with the dates the patient gave. A task carries priority, status, activity type, assignee, reporter, due date and time, an optional repeat, an associated patient, notes and files, and the list opens on what is overdue.

    On the table

    Bolt on a generic CRM and a generic to-do tool, or build leads and tasks into the clinic app where the patient record already lives.

    Why

    The people chasing leads are the same people at reception, and the practitioner reviewing a questionnaire is the same person treating the patient at one o'clock. A second tool was exactly the thing we were removing.

    What it cost

    Scope. Leads alone is 93 screens and Tasks is 39, because every dropdown, filter and confirmation needed its own state, and the create-appointment path from a lead had to handle deposits and the SMS to the patient.

    AI with named jobs, not a general assistant

    What I chose

    Four named jobs, each living inside an existing screen and each producing a draft the practitioner refines and saves. The AI tools are packaged as the Advanced add-on alongside memberships and the accounting and marketing integrations, so the core plans are not sold on AI.

    On the table

    The easy headline was an AI assistant that does a bit of everything. In a medicolegal setting that headline is a liability, because every AI output needs a named place in the record and a practitioner who reviews it before it goes in.

    Why

    Named jobs can be explained to a regulator, trained into a team and reviewed one at a time. A chat window cannot.

    What it cost

    A smaller headline on the website, and a long list of things the AI is deliberately not allowed to do.

    How it was built

    Figma, built for a machine to read

    Before any of the automation is useful the file has to be clean, and that was real design work. Every flow has a title frame, every screen is named for the state it shows, superseded work goes to an archive page rather than being deleted, and the booking file keeps desktop and mobile on parallel pages. Those were team rules rather than personal habits, so every designer's file read the same way to the machine and to the developers. The QA page is the one I would point to: screenshots of the live build from July 2024 sit directly beside the redesigned screens, so every review compared what shipped with what was designed.

    Reading the files through the Figma MCP

    Claude reads the file node by node, names, properties and structure, instead of being briefed screen by screen from screenshots. The output is an inventory of every state, and a list of mismatches between the design, the spec and the live build, before a single screen is rebuilt.

    Prototypes in code for anything with logic

    Anything with branching behaviour became one interactive HTML file a stakeholder could click through: sign-up v2 end to end, the shop flows, the booking steps. Every publish runs jsdom smoke tests, tag balance and script syntax, so the prototype behaves the same on every republish.

    The marketing site, generated from the design

    The homepage started as a Figma export. Claude rebuilt it as one self-contained HTML page with the fonts, colours and assets pulled from the design and the structure matching section for section, then it was enhanced round by round: a silk-ribbon video in the hero, a logo marquee, scroll-linked stats, micro-animations in the Smart notes panel and five fanned switching cards. The Why AesthetiDocs page added a story player, a deposit-flow animation and an FAQ written only from facts already on the page. Light and dark mode and reduced motion are respected.

    What the AI did

    The typing. It read the files, generated markup on the token set, wrote the prototype code, drafted copy against the house rules and ran the checks. It only ever writes against something I have already designed.

    What I decided

    Every call on hierarchy, responsive behaviour and interaction states. Which departures from the file to keep, what goes to the founders for sign-off, what the team and the developers get back in review, and what ships. And the house rules every output is checked against:

    Where it went wrong

    The wins are nice. These are what make the workflow believable.

    It invented a feature

    In an early pass the model added an AI co-pilot to the product. There is no such thing. I corrected it, applied the fix across every page, and wrote the rule into the brief: AesthetiDocs AI has exactly four named jobs and nothing else.

    It filled a gap with plausible copy

    On the homepage build, five FAQ answers came back as placeholders because the answers were not in the source design. They were flagged and held rather than shipped as if real. The rule became: marketing copy only from facts on the page or signed off by the stakeholder.

    It was right when the source was wrong

    Two typos in the source copy surfaced during the build. They were held for the stakeholder to confirm rather than silently corrected, because a quiet fix in a prototype makes the handoff look wrong later.

    AI inside the product follows the same restraint. The four jobs live inside existing screens rather than a chat window, each produces a draft a practitioner refines and saves, and nothing automated touches a record without a named person in between.

    Iterations

    The messy middle, screen by screen.

    None of these flows went from idea to finished screen. Each file keeps its own history: an Archive page where superseded work goes instead of being deleted, a QA page in the booking file where the developer build sits beside the design, and the founders' notes that changed what got built. This is how each module moved from its first version to the one that shipped, and why.

    Archive pages

    Every file keeps the versions it replaced: tablet layouts, three generations of the payment step, nine voucher cards, a Tasks table that began as an inventory screen.

    QA

    Screenshots of the development build placed beside the designed screen for seven desktop and three mobile steps, so the review compared what was built with what was drawn.

    Stakeholder notes

    Sticky notes on the Leads file that became product rules: the payment link by SMS, the 24-hour ghost appointment, the duration that calculates itself and can be overridden.

    Three layouts became two.

    The early versions carried a dedicated iPad portrait and iPad landscape layout alongside desktop. They are in the archive now. The desktop design already runs as a single centred column, which behaves well at tablet widths, so a third layout was maintenance without benefit. V3 put desktop and mobile on parallel pages instead, so every state is designed twice and never drifts.

    The main screen got shorter and gained a job.

    The first page a patient sees went from a long scroll to a compact one: booking information, the clinic's locations with a map and business hours, and an about section. On mobile it went through five versions, gaining and then losing a gift voucher block, and spawning separate location selection and map screens so the small screen never has to hold two decisions at once. The mobile menu grew from sign in and social links to include contact and gift vouchers.

    Treatment selection learned the clinic's reality.

    New and existing patients are separated at the top of the step, because a returning patient can book a review or a top-up that a new patient should not see. Current offers sit in a carousel above a search field, and the treatments live in accordion categories. Treatments that require a prior consultation gained an information pop-up before they can be added.

    Practitioner selection stopped being a dropdown.

    Each practitioner got a profile card with title, years of experience and training, with a full profile behind it. Next available practitioner moved to the top as an explicit first option with a line explaining that the practitioner is assigned at the appointment.

    Date and time stopped dead-ending.

    Slots are grouped into morning, afternoon and evening tabs. A day with nothing free shows the waitlist instead of an empty message, and a slot taken during the hold gets an error that says exactly that and returns the patient to the available times.

    Login stopped losing bookings.

    The login and register pop-ups gained Google and Apple sign-in, a continue-with-email path, forgot and reset password without leaving the flow, and a short remaining-details form after a Google sign-in. Booked, error, reschedule and rescheduled confirmation screens completed the set.

    The QA pass: development build beside the design

    By July 2024 the development build had the structure right: the stacked steps, the new and existing patient tabs, the offers carousel, search and accordions, the countdown, the deposit and full-payment options. What it had lost was the hierarchy. Step cards were paler and tighter, type was smaller, and the practitioner card had turned into a name and a job title. On its own that reads as polish. On a booking page it is the difference between a patient who understands what they are paying today and one who phones the clinic.

    The QA page put a screenshot of the build beside the design for the main screen, treatment selection, practitioner selection, date and time, review and payment, the waitlist and the login on desktop, and the main, treatment and practitioner screens on mobile. I sent the gaps back to the developers as a screen-by-screen list rather than a general note about fidelity.

    Self sign-up, v1 to v2

    Before

    The first version followed the research board: five sign-up fields, then four onboarding steps in a fixed sequence, then guidance inside the product. The right split in the wrong order. The plan being trialled, and the terms of the trial, were decided after the account existed, and the onboarding steps blocked the app until they were done.

    After

    Version two moves plan choice to the front and turns onboarding into a checklist. The pricing rule is explicit: you only pay for practitioners, admin and reception accounts are free, every plan starts with a 14-day trial and nothing is paid today. The clinic login address is suggested from the clinic name and checked live. The email is verified with a six-digit code, with the reason stated on screen. Enterprise clinics get a contact path instead of a card form. If the trial ends with no card, the account is paused, not deleted.

    Why

    Trials die on a blank product, a forced sequence or a surprise charge. Plan first removes the surprise. Any order respects that clinics set up differently, some from the team outwards and some from the services. Paused-not-deleted turns a lapsed trial into a lead rather than a loss.

    What the design work changed

    For patients

    A booking website where the deposit is explained before the pay step, a fully booked practitioner leads to a waitlist rather than a dead end, and a confirmed appointment carries what was paid, what is owed and a way to reschedule.

    For the front desk

    The deposit is already taken when the patient arrives, forms and consent are already done, and Kiosk check-in lets the patient check themselves in. Kiosk touches all four areas at once, which is why it got its own page on the website.

    For the team

    Leads and tasks live inside the record. A waitlist request becomes a lead with dates attached, a lead becomes an appointment with the deposit shown, a note becomes a task, and the task list opens on what is overdue.

    For new clinics

    A clinic can choose a plan, create an account, verify its email and reach a working dashboard with a five-step setup checklist without anyone from AesthetiDocs being involved.

    What I'd change

    Where AI fits, and where it doesn't.

    Measure the funnel I designed
