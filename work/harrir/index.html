A fashion marketplace with an AI stylist and a community loop

Harrir brings Turkish fashion to Iraq: a bilingual storefront on web and mobile, a stylist called Noor who answers in real products, and a community where sharing a look pays into your wallet. I designed all of it, built most of the front end myself, and set up the tools the team now uses to keep its AI improving without an engineer in the room.

    Context

    A store that holds no stock, for a market nobody had built for properly.

    Harrir sells Turkish fashion to Iraq without owning any inventory. The catalogue is listed from Turkish retailers like Trendyol, Koton, LC Waikiki, DeFacto, Mango, Shein and Hepsiburada. When a customer places an order, Harrir buys the item from the source store under that customer's name and ID, receives it at its own warehouse, checks it, and ships it on to Baghdad, Erbil, Basra, Mosul, Najaf, Sulaymaniyah or Karbala.

    That model shaped every decision. Prices are in Iraqi dinar and run into six figures for a single dress. Delivery is measured in weeks, with air and sea as real choices rather than a shipping tier. Payment runs through local wallets and banks like ZainCash, Qi Card and FIB far more than through cards. Sizes come from a different country. And every screen has to read naturally in Arabic and English, with the whole layout mirrored for RTL.

    The team was small. I was the only designer, a two-person marketing team were my main reviewers, and there was no front-end team waiting on the other side of a handoff. Three products needed designing at once: a website, a mobile app, and an admin console covering the warehouse, orders, coupons, support, analytics, notifications and the training of three separate AI systems.

    The problem in business terms

    Three problems, and each one costs money rather than feelings.

    Not "users found it confusing". These are the things a founder loses sleep over, and they are the brief I actually designed against.

    Trust

    Three unanswered questions and a six-figure price

    An Iraqi shopper buying from Turkey is guessing at three things: when the parcel arrives, whether the size will fit, and what happens if it doesn't. Add a six-figure price in dinar and a card form for someone who pays by ZainCash, and every unanswered question becomes a reason to close the tab. The business only earns when an order is placed and kept, so trust is the conversion problem wearing a different coat.

    Discovery

    A scraped catalogue is a price list, not a store

    Tens of thousands of items from seven retailers gives you range and no point of view. A grid that size does nothing for someone dressing for an Eid dinner or a graduation, and once they've bought the one thing they came for, nothing brings them back next week. Without a reason to return, growth is just a marketing bill.

    Delivery

    Web, app and admin for one designer

    One designer across three surfaces means one of two things. Either the admin console ships late and ugly, which the warehouse and support teams pay for every day, or the customer app drifts from the design each time a developer rebuilds it by eye. Both cost months. The first two problems are product problems. This one is a process problem, and it's the one automation could actually fix.

    The call

    Three forks in the road. I'd make the same call on each again.

    Every project has a few decisions that set the shape of everything after. These were Harrir's, with what each one cost.

    A storefront, a stylist app, or both

    Options

    A conventional storefront, a chat-first stylist app, or a storefront with the stylist one tap away.

    Picked

    The third. Most people arrive knowing roughly what they want, and a fast, familiar catalogue serves them best. The stylist and the feed are for everyone else, and they have to earn their tap: every answer Noor gives resolves into real products at real IQD prices with an add-to-bag button, never just advice.

    Cost

    Two surfaces to design, keep consistent and explain. And a stylist that has to be good, because a mediocre one is dead weight on the home page.

    Luxury editorial, or playful and honest

    Options

    A luxury editorial direction, or a playful, honest one.

    Picked

    Playful. The early concept frames, several of them AI-generated, pushed a dark, gold, serif "Atelier" direction organised around occasions: a Baghdad wedding, a graduation, a family iftar, a business gala. The occasion insight was right and it survived, in the onboarding quiz and in Noor’s prompts. The aesthetic was wrong. The catalogue is Trendyol and LC Waikiki, and a couture skin over mid-market prices reads as a lie the moment the price loads. Harrir went berry, purple and teal instead, with clouds, sparkles and soft corners.

    Cost

    Several polished explorations in the bin and a brand system rebuilt from the ground up. Shipping them would have cost trust instead of time.

    Hand the design off, or build it

    Options

    Redlines and a developer rebuilding every section by eye, or generating the front end myself.

    Picked

    Build it. I generated the front end with Claude, validated each file before anyone reviewed it, and pushed the result back into Figma, so the design file and the shipped screen were always the same thing. Iteration moved from sprints to hours, and the admin console, which nobody ever budgets design time for, got designed properly.

    Cost

    An unproven workflow, no one to blame for front-end bugs but me, and the discipline to keep every decision mine while the model did the typing.

    The design

    Bilingual from the first frame, built around moments, honest about distance.

    What shipped, surface by surface, and the reasoning that sits under each screen.

    Bilingual from the first frame.

    Language sits in the header next to search, one tap from anywhere. Every screen was designed twice: once in English, once in Arabic with the layout mirrored for RTL, so chevrons, progress steps and swipe directions all read the right way. Designing twice is slower. Retrofitting RTL into a finished product is much slower, and it never looks native.

    A storefront built around moments.

    The homepage opens on Eid and Ramadan campaigns, then settles into a rhythm: a campaign banner, a product row, a banner again. Flash sales, holiday essentials, top discounts, top brands, most wishlisted, an AI recommendation row and recently viewed each get their slot. Category tiles go deeper than "Clothing": tops, dresses, beachwear, hoodies, party wear, co-ords, so the first tap already feels specific. The stylist banner, "Your personal stylist, in your pocket", sits in the first scroll with its four prompt chips, so the second surface is discoverable from the first. List pages carry a full filter drawer and sort; product pages carry a size guide and the saved-size prompt.

    Checkout that's honest about distance.

    Air and sea shipping sit side by side with dated delivery windows before payment, not in a confirmation email after. Coupons and bank offers live in one place with a count, wallet credit applies as its own line and a PCI note sits under the card form for the people who are nervous about it. Payday badges mark eligible items so the total never surprises. The order summary breaks out subtotal, shipping, tax, coupon discount and wallet discount, then one number and one black button. Success, failed and cancelled each got their own screen rather than a generic error.

    Sizing saved once.

    Turkish sizing doesn't map cleanly to what Iraqi shoppers know, and a cross-border return is expensive for everyone. My Size captures clothing, bra, bralette and shoe sizes once in the profile and surfaces them on every product page, right next to the size guide.

    Returns you can see.

    Request a return, return submitted, refund processed: every state has its own screen inside Order Details, with the reason and the timeline visible. A return you can watch is a return you'll trust, and that is what makes a first order feel safe.

    A six-step style quiz.

    First run asks about body type, occasions and the brands you like, shows a short "personalising" moment, and lands you on a feed that already looks like you. Day one isn't a blank slate, for you or for Noor.

    Sign-in without a password.

    Phone or email, a verification code, three short onboarding screens. Nothing to invent and nothing to forget, which matters when most first visits happen on a phone during a sale.

    Noor, the stylist.

    The chat opens with four prompt chips so nobody faces an empty box. The photo path takes a screenshot from Instagram, Pinterest or anywhere, or a past Harrir order, so Noor can style around what you already own instead of selling you a whole new wardrobe. While she thinks you see a generating state rather than a spinner, and every reply comes back as a look with the real products underneath, each with its IQD price and an add-to-bag button. Threads save to a history you can come back to or delete. That last step, from advice to a bag, is the difference between a stylist and a chatbot.

    The feed gives the stylist a reason to come back.

    On web, chat sits on the left and the feed on the right under four tabs: For You, Harrir, Style Inspo and Community. On mobile it's two tabs, Style Chat and Feed. Posts from brands and from the community carry the products in the look as shoppable cards, Instagram reels play as shoppable stories with add-to-bag on the overlay, and search sits above it all for when you know exactly what you want. The editorial posts talk about Baghdad rather than Paris: the colours on Karrada's streets this season, what to wear to an iftar.

    My Look pays in IQD.

    Customers post a photo or a short video of a look, tag the products, and the admin team reviews it. An approved story pays points straight into the wallet that spends at checkout, with a milestone bonus early on and daily and monthly caps so it can't be gamed. The profile shows tier, verified-buyer status, featured count and an earning history, so status is visible without being loud. Linking Instagram takes four screens: how it works, connect, verify with a live rotating code you post as a comment, done. The system reads the username from whichever account comments the code, so there's nothing to type and nothing to get wrong.

    The admin console wears a different outfit.

    It has its own design system on purpose: a neutral page grey, white surfaces, Inter for text and a monospace face for numbers, with the brand colours kept for customers only. It covers the warehouse management system, orders, coupons, the support centre, conversations with topic and escalation views, customer analytics, user management and the notification centre. Filters stack as removable chips, lists react instantly, and every modal is owned by the page that opens it, which sounds like engineering and is really what makes the console feel calm at five o'clock on a sale day.

    How we built it

    Figma to Claude to a validated build to review, then back into Figma.

    Me deciding at every stage, the model doing the typing. Here's the pipeline as it actually ran, with what each stage replaced and what it saved.

    Figma

    Brand tokens, a shared component set, and every screen in both languages. The file is the real design work: messy nodes make messy code, and no prompt fixes that.

    Claude

    Short, directive prompts against the established system. One self-contained React file per surface, on Tailwind with lucide icons and Recharts for the admin charts.

    Rebuild

    Every file compiled before I looked at it, so syntax errors never reached a reviewer. Big files landed in checked chunks with line counts verified between parts.

    Review

    Every interactive element fires a toast, so the marketing team could click through a whole screen and see what each control would do. Feedback came back as short notes, not redlines.

    Back into Figma

    The html.to.design MCP imported the shipped screen as editable layers. The file and the product never drifted, and a comment in Figma landed on the real thing.

    AI inside the product

    Noor's chat and the recommendation rows run on Anthropic's Messages API, Claude Sonnet 4 series. The design job there was deciding what the model is allowed to say: a look, the real products in it, their IQD prices, and nothing it can't back with a product card.

    The boring rules that made it work

    One file per surface. A toast on every click. Modal state owned by the page, not the component. Filters that stack as chips and can be removed one at a time. None of it is glamorous, and all of it is why reviewers could trust a screen the first time they opened it.

    The AI Training Console: automation for the team, not just for me.

    Three systems need ongoing training. Harrir Loop ranks search results and runs the ML pipeline behind discovery. Noor is the stylist. Hoor is the support bot. Engineers couldn't own that forever, so I designed a console where the marketing team does it. Everything speaks plain language by default, and a Technical View toggle brings the engineering vocabulary back for anyone who wants it. Training Noor happens inside a slide-in drawer as a conversation: she asks one question at a time, you answer with quick-reply chips or text, pick a verdict, tick the issues, correct the query, draft a better reply with suggestions and a character counter, add an Arabic version if you want one, and preview before you submit. A chat-shaped system should be trained in a chat.

    What I kept

    Every layout, hierarchy and interaction call. Every locale decision, from which payment methods appear to how a date range reads in Arabic. The reward economics. What gets reorganised versus what gets removed. The brand voice. Sign-off on every file before anyone else saw it.

    What the AI did

    First drafts of every screen. The typing, which is most of what building a front end is. Volume in concept exploration: dozens of directions in the time one used to take.

    Where AI got it wrong

    Asked to simplify an admin page, it stripped functionality instead of restructuring it, and the note back was the same every time: reorganise, don't remove. The ML interfaces kept arriving in ML jargon, and plain language took several cycles to become the default rather than a patch. Concept generation defaulted to luxury, which looked impressive and was wrong for the catalogue. All three were caught in review. None would have been caught by the model.

    Iterations

    Four changes after feedback, each a before and after.

    Real product design rarely goes from idea to final screen in a straight line. These are the turns that mattered.

    The stylist grew a feed.

    Version one was a standalone chat: upload a photo, write a prompt, get a response. It worked, and nobody had a reason to open it twice. The current version pairs chat with the feed, My Look, search and rewards on one surface, so Noor answers the question you came with and the feed gives you one to come back for.

    The training pipeline lost a column.

    The first status flow had three stages, New, Training and Final, joined by pipeline arrows. Reviewers read the arrows as something they had to operate. It collapsed to two columns, New and Final, with training status as a property on each row. Less diagram, more table, faster to read.

    Noor training went from wizard to conversation.

    The first Train flow was a multi-step form. It was correct and it felt like filing a ticket. Rebuilding it as a chat, with edit and rewind on each answer, made training feel like the thing being trained, and the reviewers finished a session without asking how.

    Instagram verification stopped asking.

    Version one asked for a username and showed a static code. Version two shows a live rotating code and reads the username from whichever account comments it. One fewer field, one fewer way to get it wrong, and one fewer thing for support to untangle.

    Because the front end was generated rather than rebuilt by hand, each of these went from feedback to a reviewable build in four hours instead of waiting for the next sprint. Three rounds in a day where there used to be three in a sprint is the product outcome of the pipeline, not a side effect.

    The team can train its own AI.

    Search ranking, the stylist and the support bot are improved by marketing staff in plain language, with no engineer in the loop. That's the automation that compounds: every week of use makes the product better without a ticket, and the people closest to customers are the ones teaching it.

    Design and build never drifted.

    Every shipped screen exists in Figma as the real thing, not a drawing of it, because the code went back into the file. The admin console, which usually gets whatever time is left, got the same care as the storefront.

    It feels like a local store.

    Arabic that reads as Arabic, dinar prices, ZainCash at checkout, delivery windows you can plan around, and a feed that talks about Karrada rather than Milan. None of that is a feature. All of it is why someone trusts a first order.

    What I'd change

    Next time, and the time after.

    What I'd automate next, what I'd keep by hand, and what I learned about where AI fits in design work and where it doesn't.

    1. Put Iraqi payment methods first in checkout.ZainCash, Qi Card and FIB ahead of the card form, because that's how the market actually pays, and a card-first screen quietly tells a wallet user this store isn't for them.
    2. Make the Figma file machine-readable from day one.Named nodes, auto layout everywhere, one frame per state and no default frame names. Every unnamed layer is a worse prompt, and both files still carry too many of them.
    3. Automate QA next.A browser agent walking every flow at mobile and desktop widths, in both languages, flagging anything that doesn't match the frame. RTL regressions are the ones that slip past a human on a Friday afternoon.
    4. Keep two things manual: the reward economics and the copy.Points, caps and bonuses need a human who understands the margin, and the voice of a playful Iraqi fashion brand is not something I'd let a model set.
    5. Instrument the stylist properly.The number that matters isn't how many people opened Noor, it's how many conversations ended in a bag. That number decides whether the second surface earned its tap, and I'd want it on the admin home page.
