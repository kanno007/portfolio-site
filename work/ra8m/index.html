    Context

    One system, two very different users.

    RA8M is a commerce platform for businesses in Iraq. The storefront is its customer-facing side, and it runs on the same system that handles inventory and orders. What a business sets up in the back office is what shoppers see in the shop.

    Who it is for

    The sellers RA8M is built for are small businesses in Iraq that already sell online, just not through a shop. Most of them run on Instagram and Facebook: a product goes up as a post or a story, customers ask for the price in the comments or a DM, the seller confirms size and colour over chat or a phone call, writes the order down, and sends it with cash on delivery. It works, but every sale is a conversation, nothing is tracked, and the shop is only as available as the person holding the phone.

    RA8M gives those sellers the next step without asking them to become a tech company. The storefront is their own shop with the catalogue, variants, cart and checkout already in place, and it is the kind of link they can drop into a bio or a story. The admin puts orders, statuses, coupons and stock in one place instead of a notebook and a chat thread. The social accounts keep doing what they are good at, which is bringing people in, and the shop does the selling.

    What shaped the work

    Two very different users

    A shopper on a phone and an operator at a desk share one product. The storefront has to feel light. The admin has to carry tables, filters and forms without feeling like a different company.

    Products come with variants

    Sizes, colours, SKUs and barcodes are part of how inventory is structured, so the interface had to follow that model all the way from the product card to the order row.

    Built to be reused

    The timeline meant every screen had to be reusable rather than one-off. The sample catalogue is skincare and beauty, but nothing in the structure depends on it.

    The problem

    A business that wants to sell online usually hits the same three walls.

    Wall 01

    Buying has too many steps

    A product page that throws you out of the list you were browsing, or a checkout that asks for more than it needs, loses sales without anyone noticing.

    Wall 02

    The owner's work is scattered

    Orders in one place, discounts in another, stock somewhere else. Launching a coupon on Monday can mean three tools and a spreadsheet.

    Wall 03

    Every surface drifts

    If web, mobile and admin each get their own components, every change gets paid for three times, and the product slowly stops looking like one product.

    The goal

    Make buying take as few decisions as possible, make the admin follow the same data model as the storefront, and build all of it once, on one system.

    The call

    Four decisions shaped the product more than anything else.

    Each one is shown here as it was built, so you can see what it feels like, not just read about it.

    Decision 01

    Product detail opens in a drawer, not a new page

    The usual route is a dedicated product page. I kept the shopper in the grid instead. A 544px drawer slides in from the right while the categories and product list stay visible behind it. People compare as they shop, and the next product is always one tap away.

    The same drawer holds the cart, in three states: product detail, filled cart and empty cart. Shoppers learn one pattern and I maintain one component. The cost is space, so detail content is cut into short blocks with quantity and Add to Cart pinned at the bottom. On mobile the drawer becomes a full screen with its own header.

    Decision 02

    Checkout is one page

    The order summary sits on one side. Address, coupon code and payment sit on the other, with a single Complete Order button. I chose this over a stepped flow because the form is short, and a stepper would add screens without adding clarity.

    Name, phone, city and address, an optional coupon code, then card or cash. A confirmation modal closes the loop with the order ID, so there is no separate success page to design or keep in sync.

    Decision 03

    Coupons are organised by what the merchant wants to do

    Instead of one long form with every option on it, the merchant first picks what they want to do: amount off products, amount off the order, buy X get Y, or free shipping. The form then shows only the fields that type needs: code or automatic, value, what it applies to, minimum requirements, usage limits and dates.

    That meant designing more than 30 states, but every screen stays short and hard to get wrong. Change the type and the fields change with it, while the layout stays put.

    Decision 04

    Order status and payment status are tracked separately

    An order can be Pending while payment is Paid, or Delivered while payment has Failed. One merged status would hide both situations.

    The order list shows the two side by side: nine fulfilment statuses from Pending to Delivered, plus Return and Cancelled, next to three payment states. The operator reads the row once and knows what to do.

    The shopping journey

    Three moments, from the first scroll to the back office that fulfils the order.

    Browse

    The home screen opens with a hero banner, then drops straight into the catalogue. Scrolling collapses the hero and pins the header with the category tabs right under it, so filtering is always one tap away.

    The web grid shows five cards across and mobile shows two. Each card comes in three flavours, default, discount and offer tag, so a label like BOGO is part of the component rather than pasted on later.

    Buy

    Opening a product shows a gallery with thumbnails, name and price, then size and colour pickers, a short description table and a shipping note. The footer changes from Add to Cart to Item Added to Checkout, so the next step is always the obvious one.

    Checkout puts everything on one screen: summary, name, phone, city and address, an optional coupon, and a choice of card or cash. A confirmation modal closes the loop with the order ID.

    Run the shop

    The admin shares the storefront's visual language. A sidebar groups work into Analytics, Order Management, Inventory, Sales, Purchases, Finance, Reports and Settings, with accordions so the menu stays calm.

    The order list supports search, filters, bulk selection, a quick action menu and pagination. Opening an order shows both statuses, the items, customer and address details, shipping and payment tabs, and a timeline. Coupon Settings walks the merchant through type, method, value, scope, requirements, limits and dates.

    How I built it

    System first, screens second.

    I built the foundations as a shared library instead of per screen: 40 colour variables in four families, each with a 50 to 900 ramp, and Visby CF as the product typeface. On top sits a small component set, buttons in three sizes with primary, secondary, outline and disabled types, plus inputs, checkboxes, radios and category tabs.

    Then the bigger pieces: product card, header, the drawer, and an admin set with sidebar accordions, status chips, filters, and the order, item, bill and coupon blocks. States live inside the components as variants. The header has a before and after scroll state, the card has default, discount and offer tag, and the drawer has product, filled cart and empty cart, so nothing is redrawn per screen.

    A file made of named components and variables is also something a machine can read. Through the Figma MCP, an AI sees component names, variants and variable names instead of guessing from a screenshot, which makes it useful for review, design-to-code and repetitive production work.

    What the review flagged, before the rebuild

    A solid start, but a few gaps made the system harder to use, scale and maintain.

    Inconsistent structure

    Patterns and layouts varied across screens.

    Unclear colour usage

    Colours lacked a defined purpose and consistency.

    Incomplete component states

    Key states, hover, active, disabled and empty, were missing.

    Limited scalability

    The setup was not flexible for future updates.

    Harder to maintain

    No shared library, clear naming or guidelines.

    What I would change

    Four things I would do next.

    The storefront and the admin are complete, but a few things would make the product stronger.

    Arabic and right-to-left

    RA8M is built for Iraq and the header already has a language switch, but every screen is English today. I would pair Visby CF with an Arabic typeface, mirror the layouts, and test long Arabic strings in the cart and the admin tables.

    The unhappy paths

    The happy path is well covered. An invalid or expired coupon, a declined payment, an out-of-stock variant and form errors are not, and those are the moments where trust is won or lost.

    Money, shown the same way everywhere

    Shipping only appears at checkout, and the admin order total leaves out shipping and discounts. I would show an estimate in the cart and give the admin the same breakdown the customer sees.

    Finish the system

    Bring the admin screens to the same token coverage as the storefront, and add text styles so type comes from the system too.
