A specialty pharmacy refill in two taps, not five

WellDyneRX is a US specialty pharmacy. Its patient portal, WellView, is where patients manage prescriptions, refills and orders, and it was failing them: a usability score in the "poor" range and six in ten checkouts abandoned. I was the solo designer on the redesign, across web and mobile, for patients, doctors and pharmacy administrators.

Role
Lead UX Designer, solo designer
Duration
5 months
Platforms
Web portal and mobile app for patients, doctors and pharmacy administrators
Responsibilities

In thirty seconds

The problem

Three of four interviewees had missed a refill in the past six months. Expiry dates sat three clicks deep, ordering took four separate screens, there were no reminders, and the baseline SUS was 58. Support calls went up and adherence went down.

What I did

Four interviews and an SUS survey, affinity mapping, a competitive read, four principles, then a redesign that surfaces what is urgent on the home screen, folds ordering and tracking into the portal, and adds reminders.

What changed

Refill requests went from five taps to two, four of five testers rated the new flow "very easy" against one of five before, and SUS rose from 58 to 71.1, above the healthcare benchmark of 68.

    Context

    Four problems, each with a cost the pharmacy could feel.

    Specialty pharmacy is heavily regulated, and the job was to make the prescription-to-delivery experience feel seamless and trustworthy for patients while giving doctors and pharmacists the tools to manage prescriptions. The objective was plain: simplify and secure obtaining, refilling and managing prescriptions, with an e-commerce flow for medication delivery built in. The legacy portal was getting in the way on four fronts.

    Problem

    Complex prescription management

    Three of four interviewees had missed at least one refill in the past six months. The legacy system buried expiry dates three clicks deep and the baseline SUS was 58, "poor". Support call volume was up and adherence was down.

    Solution

    Patients view, manage and refill prescriptions through one simple, secure interface. Doctors prescribe directly in the platform, with real-time visibility of each patient's prescription status.

    Problem

    Poor e-commerce integration

    Ordering a medication meant navigating four separate screens. Average checkout abandonment was 60%. P2 described the old system as solving "every problem that Optum's portal put in front of me", which says a lot about the bar.

    Solution

    An e-commerce flow inside the portal: purchase medications, manage orders and track deliveries without leaving.

    Problem

    No proactive support for adherence

    The legacy system had no reminders. People relied on personal calendars or memory. P1 and P3 both described "scrambling" when they realised a prescription had expired.

    Solution

    Automated reminders for medication intake, refills and upcoming prescriptions keep people on schedule.

    Problem

    A compliant structure

    The e-commerce experience had to be both compliant and easy to use, which is a harder combination in pharmacy than in retail.

    Solution

    Order history, refill patterns and a reminder system for medication timing, inside a flow that respects the approval steps.

    What came out of it

    Insight 1

    Ease of use

    People like being able to request refills and manage prescriptions in the app, specifically on their phone.

    Insight 2

    Primary use cases

    The app is used to order refills, renew a prescription, or check the status of an order. Almost nothing else.

    Insight 3

    Information hierarchy

    The hierarchy forced people to search and navigate through the app to find what they needed.

    Insight 4

    Detailed information

    People wanted more detail at every point in the process, not less.

    Insight 5

    Customisation

    Each user has different needs depending on their benefits and prescriptions.

    Affinity mapping

    Every finding, quote and observation went on its own note, then into clusters. Three themes carried most of the weight.

    Time-sensitive information

    • I need to know immediately if something is expiring
    • The refill date is buried too deep
    • I missed a refill because I didn't see the date

    Order tracking frustration

    • I can't tell where my order actually is
    • No carrier information visible
    • I have to call to find out shipping status

    Personalisation gaps

    • Everyone's needs are different
    • I only use mail order but see PBM stuff everywhere
    • The app shows me things I don't care about

    What the competition does

    I looked at Capsule, Alto Pharmacy and the CVS app, focusing on prescription management, order tracking and notifications. The key finding: all three surface time-sensitive information, expiring prescriptions and refill due dates, on the home screen. WellView buried it.

    Principles

    Four rules, written before any screens.

    Surface urgency

    Expiring prescriptions and refill dates should never require navigation to find. People see the critical dates the moment they open the app.

    Transparency builds trust

    Every prescription status gets a clear, jargon-free explanation. People were confused by "RX pharmacy review" and "on hold".

    Reduce cognitive load

    Consolidate what people need together. Order tracking shows carrier, dates and status in one view, no extra clicks.

    Respect user diversity

    Each person has different benefits and usage patterns. The interface adapts to show what is relevant to them.

    The journey

    From onboarding through managing prescriptions, checking order status and researching a prescription, mapped with what the person is doing, how it feels, the insights and the opportunities at each step.

    What I did

    Three recommendations, each tied to a quote.

    Dashboard: match the mental model

    Restructure the home page to reflect how people think and reorder the hierarchy around what they find most important. P1 and P3 both missed refills because expiry dates were "buried"; P4 said "I only care about what's urgent". So prescriptions and orders become the purpose of the home page, and the prescriptions widget shows refills remaining.

    Design decision. Smart sorting surfaces time-sensitive items first (expiring within 30 days, ready for refill) and moves completed or expired prescriptions into a collapsible "Past" section. Less to read at the moment that matters.

    Prescriptions: say what the status means

    Since prescriptions are what people come for, they lead the home screen. The second recommendation was more detail about the process: what happens from the moment WellDyneRX receives a prescription, what "pharmacy review" means, what "on hold" means, through to tracking.

    Orders: tracking in the row, not on a details page

    Add the tracking detail to the main rows so nobody has to open a details page: a progress bar with dates, departure date (estimated and actual), arrival date (estimated and actual), the carrier's name, and a tracking number that links out.

    Design decision. Order cards expanded to include the carrier logo, estimated against actual dates and a clickable tracking number. The progress bar shows five stages instead of three, with hover states explaining each one.

    Wireframes first

    Low fidelity before any visual design: the dashboard, cart, checkout steps and prescriptions, tested as grey boxes so the conversation stayed on structure.

    How we checked

    Five people, four tasks, and a score above the benchmark.

    Moderated usability testing with five participants on the high-fidelity prototype. Each was asked to find when their Hizentra prescription expires, request a refill for Atenolol, track their most recent order, and update their shipping address.

    What changed

    Refills in two taps, and a home screen people noticed.

    Patient satisfaction

    Four of five testers rated the new refill process "very easy", compared with one of five on the legacy system. The home screen redesign came up most often as the biggest change.

    A shorter refill flow

    Requesting a refill went from five taps to two. Doctor efficiency was not measured directly, but replacing jargon with plain-language status should cut clarification calls from patients.

    Doctor efficiency

    Doctors found the direct prescription feature reduced administrative work and made managing and monitoring patient medications simpler.

    Mobile

    The mobile experience was built out for the patients who prefer managing prescriptions on their phone, which the research said was most of them.

    What I'd do differently

    What I learned, and what I would change.

    What I learned

    HIPAA changes the process, not just the product. A regulated environment meant extra documentation and approval steps. I learned to build compliance checkpoints into the design process rather than bolt them on.

    Show, don't tell. Video clips from user interviews were far more persuasive with stakeholders than synthesis documents.

    Legacy integration is a design constraint. Balancing what people needed against what the legacy system could do shaped more decisions than I expected.

    What I would change

    Test earlier, rougher. Usability testing on lower-fidelity prototypes would have caught issues before visual design.

    Push harder for baselines. Better metrics before design started would have made the impact clearer to measure.

    Recruit wider. The sample skewed toward power users.

    Where it goes next

    Personalisation by benefit type (Mail Order, PBM or both), a proactive notification system for prescription renewals, and integration with the doctor portal so prescription requests flow without a phone call.
