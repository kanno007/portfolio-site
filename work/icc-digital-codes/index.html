Building codes you can search in 14 seconds

ICC Digital Codes Premium puts the International Code Council's code books, commentaries and standards on the phones of architects, engineers and inspectors. The app had the content and a 2.8-star rating. I was the solo UX designer on a three-week redesign of the native iOS and Android app.

Industry
Construction tech, building codes
Product
ICC Digital Codes Premium, the mobile app for the International Code Council's code books
Role
Lead UX Designer, solo
Timeline
3 weeks
Platform
Android and iOS, native
Contributions
Usability audit and heuristic evaluation, session recording and survey analysis, information architecture, visual design and UI specifications
In thirty seconds

The problem

Session recordings showed experienced professionals failing at basic tasks: search that could not tell titles from content, no back button, offline downloads nobody could find, and logins that failed on the first try. Support tickets about navigation and search ran at 340 a month.

What I did

A heuristic audit, stakeholder interviews, session recordings and a survey, then four insights that set the objectives. Rebuilt the information architecture around intent, made search contextual, added a persistent back button, surfaced offline and concurrent access, and added Face ID.

What changed

Average time to find content fell from 34 seconds to 14. The App Store rating went from 2.8 to 4.4, support tickets dropped 74%, and subscription renewals rose from 67% to 82%.

    Context

    Valuable content, and an app that got in the way of it.

    For architects, engineers and inspectors working on site, quick access to accurate code information is not a convenience. It decides whether a detail passes inspection. ICC Digital Codes Premium had the content: code books, commentaries and regulatory documents. But session recordings showed even experienced professionals struggling with basic tasks, and the support queue reflected the frustration.

    How might we help building professionals quickly find and reference the code information they need, whether on a construction site or in the office?

    The core question for the redesign

    What the business needed

    1. Fewer support ticketsAbout navigation and search
    2. More use of key featuresOffline downloads and concurrent access
    3. Better ratings and retentionApp Store rating and subscription renewals
    4. A current, well-behaved appUX best practice and up-to-date visual design
    How I found out

    An audit, two stakeholder sessions, five hours of recordings and a survey.

    What else the recordings showed

    Search

    • Half of all users came to the app and went straight to "Search content"
    • People searched content far more than titles
    • Searching inside a title jumped to results from every subscribed title, and people got lost
    • Almost nobody used the left dropdown to narrow a search
    • Average search took three or four steps to reach a result

    Navigation

    • More than 60% tapped "Recently viewed", partly because it was the first section
    • Nobody tapped "View entire library"
    • Very few tapped "Favorites"
    • People browsed titles outside their subscription and could not open them

    Access and notes

    • Many could not log in on the first try, probably a forgotten password
    • 46.7% said they use Notes, but no recording showed anyone using it
    • Offline downloads and concurrent access were rarely found

    What people said

    "I don't know how to use the app. I need tutorials.""Make the search function more intuitive with filters.""I need a back button. It's very frustrating to navigate without that.""It gives me access to all my code books including commentaries. I just wish finding things was easier."
    Insights

    Four things the research made obvious.

    Insight 1

    Search is the product

    Half of all users come to the app to search. Confusing dropdowns, slow results and no sense of context made search the app's biggest liability.

    Insight 2

    Context is everything

    Search inside a code book should return results from that book. Jumping to global results breaks the mental model and wastes time.

    Insight 3

    Navigation must be predictable

    No back button and inconsistent patterns created anxiety. People need to know they can always get back to where they were.

    Insight 4

    Features don't exist if people can't find them

    Offline downloads and concurrent access were buried. A feature nobody knows about provides no value.

    What I did

    Restructure around intent, then fix the six things that hurt most.

    Information architecture

    The existing IA mixed content types with user tasks in ways that did not match how people thought about the app. The hierarchy was flat in some places and deeply nested in others. I restructured it around user intent rather than content type: search, profile, notes, concurrent access and downloads as the main branches, with the library reached through search and recently viewed rather than a separate destination.

    The six improvements

    ProblemSolutionExpected impact
    Confusing search with no contextContextual search that adapts to where the person isFaster task completion, less frustration
    No back navigationPersistent back button and breadcrumb contextPeople never feel lost
    Hidden featuresProminent placement and onboarding tooltipsHigher feature adoption
    Login failuresBiometric authenticationHigher first-attempt success
    No guidance for new usersInteractive onboarding flowFaster time to value
    Slow searchTechnical optimisation with engineeringRetrieval from 3 to 4 seconds to under 1

    Before and after

    The old home screen led with a marketing banner and "View entire library", which nobody tapped. The new one leads with search, continue reading and recently viewed, and puts the library behind the things people actually use.

    Wireframes

    Low fidelity first, so the conversations with stakeholders stayed on structure: where search lives, how results split, what a title screen contains, and what the app says when there is no result or no subscription.

    What I'd do differently

    What worked, what I would change, and what stuck.

    What worked

    Session recordings over surveys. The recordings showed behaviour that surveys could not: people who said they used Notes, and never did.

    Aligning on the research first. Once stakeholders had seen the evidence, design decisions were easy to defend.

    Treating search as the core product. Fixing the highest-impact problem first made everything after it simpler.

    What I would change

    Bring engineering in earlier. Backend constraints surfaced late and changed what was feasible.

    Set up analytics before launch. They were added afterwards, which limited the baseline we could measure against.

    Test with inspectors, not only architects and engineers. They would have raised contrast and touch-target needs sooner.

    Document the components. Reusable patterns were not written down, and inconsistencies crept in later.

    What stuck with me

    Small fixes can have outsized impact. A back button sounds trivial. For this audience it turned the app from frustrating to functional.

    Discoverability is a design problem. Great features provide no value if people do not know they exist. Visibility is a UX responsibility, not a marketing one.

    Research is the fastest path to alignment. Every hour of evidence saved several hours of debate. Opinions became hypotheses to test rather than positions to defend.

    Design for the context, not just the user. These users are not at desks. They are on sites, in basements, on rooftops. Offline access, sunlight-readable screens and quick retrieval mattered as much as the tasks themselves.
