    Overview

    A platform that had outgrown its own interface.

    The promise is simple to say. A shop owner pays one monthly price and stops juggling ten or twenty subscriptions. Customers, stock, invoices, phone calls and campaigns all live in one place, and the data moves between them without anyone retyping it.

    The product already did most of this. What it lacked was a shared language. Each module had been built when it was needed, so each one behaved a little differently. A table in Inventory did not work like a table in CRM. The whole screen was painted brand orange, so nothing on it stood out. New merchants signed up for a platform and met forty separate tools.

    My job was to make the connection visible. That meant a new navigation model, a component library the whole team could build from, and a calmer interface that could roll out without pulling the old one from under people who used it every day.

    The challenge

    Three problems hiding inside one brief.

    The brief said “new UI”. Read literally, that is a reskin. Once I had walked the product end to end, it sorted itself into three questions the design had to answer.

    Problem 1 · Findability

    How might an owner find the one tool they need among forty-five?

    A florist needs invoices, gift cards and a booking page. A repair shop needs jobs, estimates and routes. Both were looking at the same long menu, most of which they would never open.

    Problem 2 · Consistency

    How might every module teach the next one?

    Breadth is only a strength if learning Inventory makes CRM easier. With every screen behaving its own way, each new module cost the merchant a fresh learning curve.

    Problem 3 · Change

    How might we replace the interface without stopping anyone’s day?

    Merchants run their tills and payroll on this. A redesign that lands overnight is a support queue. The rollout had to be as designed as the screens.

    Discovery

    An audit first, then decisions.

    This was a redesign of a working product with a clear direction from the business, so discovery was practical. I mapped what existed before drawing anything new. Four findings shaped the rest of the work.

    What I found

    Orange was doing every job at once.

    Sidebar, cards, table rows and buttons were all the same brand orange. A primary action had nothing left to stand out with, and status colours fought the background.

    →
    What it changed

    Orange moved from surface to signal. Content sits on white, the canvas is a quiet grey, and orange is kept for the active item and the next action.

    What I found

    The same idea had many names and many layouts.

    Lists, filters, pagination and row actions were rebuilt module by module. Small differences added up to a product that felt like several.

    →
    What it changed

    One table pattern, one form pattern, one modal pattern. If a merchant has used one list in Universell, they have used all of them.

    What I found

    Half the product is about catching something from outside.

    Payments, leads, tickets, bookings, donations and event sales all start with a form or link a customer fills in. Each had its own setup flow.

    →
    What it changed

    They became one family, the Catchers, with the same four steps to create, preview, share and track.

    What I found

    New accounts opened onto a blank dashboard.

    Nothing told a new merchant what to do first, and the features that create value need setup: a payment profile, an email sender, a first product.

    →
    What it changed

    Onboarding moved inside the product: a setup checklist on the homepage, step-by-step guides per task, and a Product Map of everything on offer.

    Who it serves

    Four people, four very different screens.

    01Owner

    The owner-operator

    Runs a shop, salon or service business. Does the selling, the stock and the marketing, often on the same afternoon.

    NeedsTo get in, do one thing and get back to customers.
    02Staff

    The employee

    Has a role and a handful of screens. Takes calls, books appointments, rings up sales, counts stock.

    NeedsOnly the tools their role allows, with no noise around them.
    03Reseller

    The reseller or admin

    Packages Universell into plans and sells it on. Works in the console that sits above merchant accounts.

    NeedsTo turn a long feature list into plans a buyer can compare.
    04Customer

    The merchant’s customer

    Never logs in. Books a haircut, pays an invoice or sends a support request through a page that carries the merchant’s name.

    NeedsIt to be obvious, fast and trustworthy on any device.
    Structure

    Ten doors, and a shelf for the ones you use most.

    The sidebar groups everything by the job a merchant is doing. It has two states. Expanded shows labels for people still learning the product. Collapsed is an icon rail that hands the width back to tables and charts.

    Quick Links is the part I care about most. Every sub-menu item has a small add button, so a merchant can pin the five screens they live in and ignore the rest. The product stays broad and each person’s copy of it stays small.

    Design decisions

    Four calls, and what each one cost.

    Decision 1

    Orange is a signal now, no longer a surface.

    The old screens put white text on orange everywhere. It was unmistakably Universell and tiring to read for a full working day. In the new UI the brand colour marks three things only: where you are, what you can do next, and what needs attention.

    Status finally works too. A Pending pill reads as amber and Blocked reads as red because neither is competing with an orange row.

    The cost

    Less brand on every screen. I kept the full orange theme as an option so the identity did not vanish for people who liked it.

    Decision 2

    Ship the new UI behind a switch.

    The cheap road was a launch date. I designed a Try out new UI entry point and a Switch back to Legacy UI button in the header of every redesigned screen. Merchants choose their moment, and nobody learns a new layout in the middle of a busy Saturday.

    It also let the team release module by module. A finished Inventory did not have to wait for an unfinished CRM.

    The cost

    Two interfaces live at once, and a product that looks half-renovated for a while. Worth it for a tool people run payroll on.

    Decision 3

    Put the customer at the centre of the data.

    “Connected” is an empty word until a screen proves it. The customer profile is that screen. Calls, texts, orders, invoices, agreements, tickets, deals and reward points for one person sit behind one set of tabs, and the action row above starts any of them without leaving the page.

    The cost

    Twelve tabs is a lot. I kept them in two visible rows so nothing is buried in an overflow menu.

    Decision 4

    Teach inside the product.

    The homepage opens with a setup checklist and a progress bar. Each task links to a guide that walks through the real screens, and a QuickStart panel can sit beside the work while the merchant does it. The Product Map shows the whole platform as a tree, so people can see what they have without reading a manual.

    The cost

    A lot of screens that are only seen once. They are also the ones that decide whether a trial account becomes a paying one.

    The product

    One platform. Different problems. Thoughtful solutions.

    01Marketing

    Campaigns that run themselves

    Marketing covers CRM, email and SMS campaigns, rewards, gift cards and coupons. The piece I am proudest of is the automation builder, which had to make branching logic feel safe for someone who has never seen a flowchart.

    • Blocks are sorted the way people think: what starts it, what to check, what to do, when to stop.
    • Every card states its own settings in plain words, so the flow reads top to bottom like a sentence.
    • Starter recipes such as a welcome series or a follow-up for quiet leads mean nobody begins from an empty canvas.
    02Inventory and Analytics

    Stock you can read at a glance

    Inventory handles items, categories, modifiers, vendors, purchase orders, counts and alerts. Analytics sits on top and answers the questions an owner asks out loud.

    • Nine number tiles open the page, each with a short explanation behind its info icon.
    • Top five, weakest five and margin are a click apart, with the same chart and table toggle everywhere.
    • Every report exports to PDF or image from the same button in the same corner.
    03Communications

    A phone system without the phone company

    Calls, voicemail, SMS, email and team chat share one hub. The hard part was regulation. US carriers require SMS registration before a business can text customers, and merchants had no idea where they stood.

    • Each number shows what it can do and who answers it.
    • Registration status is a plain coloured pill, with the next step one click away.
    • Buying, assigning and forwarding a number all happen in the same table.
    04Business Operations

    The paperwork, and the page customers see

    Business Operations is invoices, estimates, orders, agreements, bookings and employees. It is also where the merchant’s own customers meet the product, so the public pages are stripped right back and carry the merchant’s brand.

    • Booking is three steps: pick services, pick a person and time, pay.
    • The voice assistant turns a spoken or uploaded instruction into a draft invoice the owner reviews before sending.
    05Tickets

    Support that starts with a form

    Tickets gives a small team a proper help desk. The Ticket Catcher is the newest member of the Catcher family and shows the pattern at its simplest.

    • A short banner explains the feature in two sentences and links to a walkthrough.
    • Active and deactivated forms share one list with one toggle.
    • The count of tickets received is a link straight to those tickets.
    Iterations

    The interface got quieter in three rounds.

    I did not arrive at the light UI in one move. The first version of the new library kept the orange surfaces and only fixed structure. Seeing real forms and dashboards in it is what pushed the next two rounds.

    Round 1

    New structure, old paint

    Consistent cards, fields and spacing, still on orange. Lists looked fine. Long forms did not. White outlined inputs on an orange card are hard to scan and placeholder text nearly disappears.

    Kept: the layout grid and card structure.
    Round 2

    White tiles on orange

    On the dashboard I tried lifting the important things onto white tiles. It helped the tiles and made everything around them feel heavier. The checklist below was the tell: a wall of orange with thin white type.

    Kept: the Quick Links tiles and the checklist itself.
    Round 3

    Flip the ratio

    White cards, grey canvas, a white sidebar with an orange marker on the active item. The same checklist now reads in seconds, and the one orange button on the screen is the thing to press.

    Shipped as the default, with orange and dark as themes.
    AI and automation

    Help that does the work, with the owner still in charge.

    Automation builder

    Four kinds of block, nothing else to learn

    Marketing automation can get complicated fast. I limited the vocabulary to four block types and wrote each one as a plain statement about a customer.

    TriggersNew lead added, deal created, link clicked, birthday, abandoned cart
    ConditionsOpened email, has a deal, in a segment, no activity since
    ActionsSend email, send SMS, wait, add to segment
    GoalsThe point where the customer leaves the flow

    Test, pause and log screens sit next to the builder. People only trust automation they can stop.

    AI features

    One rule: AI drafts, the merchant confirms

    The voice assistant listens to “invoice Jane for two table repairs” and fills in the invoice. It never sends anything on its own. A Live Activity panel shows what it understood, and the Create Invoice button stays disabled until there is something to review.

    AI usage is metered in credits with its own screen, so cost is visible before it is spent.

    Outcome

    One product where there used to be many.

    The redesign now runs across the platform: Activity, Marketing, Inventory, Analytics, Tickets, Communications, Business Operations and the console, all drawn from the same library. A merchant who learns one table has learned every table. A developer who builds one form has the parts for the next.

    For merchants

    A homepage that says what to do first, a sidebar they can shape with Quick Links, and a customer record that shows the whole relationship.

    For the team

    A shared library with every state drawn, so new features start from components and reviews are about the flow.

    For the business

    A product that looks like the single platform it sells, and a rollout that never forced a customer to switch before they were ready.

    Reflection

    What I would do differently.

    01

    Name things once, early

    A few labels drifted while modules were designed in parallel. Messenger and Team Messaging are the same place. On a platform this wide, a shared glossary on day one is cheaper than a clean-up later.

    02

    Start with role-based tokens

    The palette began as named colours and grew roles afterwards. Dark theme would have been faster if every colour had been defined by its job from the start.

    03

    Watch the switch

    The legacy toggle is a free research tool. Next time I would instrument it from the first release and let the screens people switch back from set the priority list.
