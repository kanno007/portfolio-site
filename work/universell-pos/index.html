Universell POS puts sales, payments, inventory, customers, staff and reporting into one app that works on a phone at a market stall and on a tablet at a supermarket lane. This is how it went from a long requirement list to a product a cashier can learn in one shift.

    Overview

    A till that had to fit three very different shops.

    Universell is a business platform for merchants. The POS is the part of it that lives on the counter, and it ships next to Stellar payments. So the app has two jobs: take money quickly, and help a new merchant become a payments customer without feeling sold to.

    I led the design across sign-up, onboarding, checkout, inventory look-up, orders, settings and end-of-day reporting, on a shared component library that also carries the kiosk, ticketing, inventory count and label printing apps.

    The challenge

    Three problems hiding inside one brief.

    The brief arrived as a list of features. Read as a list, it would have produced a crowded app. I grouped it into the three outcomes the business actually needed, and wrote each as a question the design had to answer.

    Problem 1 · Speed

    How might one home screen serve a stall, a boutique and a supermarket?

    A stall types a price. A boutique taps a photo. A supermarket scans, and wants to see far more than five items at once. One fixed layout makes at least two of them slower.

    Problem 2 · Trust

    How might an owner close the day without doubting the numbers?

    The report had to work on screen, on a thermal roll and on A4, and say the same thing in all three. An owner who recounts the drawer by hand has stopped trusting the app.

    Problem 3 · Growth

    How might sign-up create a payments lead without scaring a new merchant?

    Every sign-up should land in Universell as a lead tagged for POS. The merchant, though, came to sell something today, and a payments application on minute one is a reason to leave.

    Research

    What I looked at before drawing anything.

    This was a redesign with a clear direction from the business, so discovery was short and practical. Four inputs shaped the work.

    Input A

    Brief audit

    Every line of the brief went onto a board in Figma with a status. Sorting the lines by outcome is what exposed the three problems above.

    Input B

    Competitor teardown

    I walked the ring-up, payment and report flows of the US apps the team named as benchmarks, including GoDaddy POS and ShopKeep.

    Input C

    Platform review

    The Universell web platform already had a sales report merchants knew. The app report had to feel like its sibling.

    Input D

    Flow mapping

    I mapped the old app screen by screen, from splash to receipt, and marked every point where a cashier had to stop and read.

    Who it serves

    Three counters, two roles.

    I skipped named personas. For a till, the shape of the counter predicts behaviour better than age or attitude, so I designed against three counters and the two people who log in.

    The stall

    One person, one phone, no catalogue. Prices live in their head. They need a keypad, a total and a way to take cash in under ten seconds.

    PhoneCustom amountCash first

    The boutique

    A few hundred items that staff recognise by sight. They sell the same twenty things most days and want those one tap away.

    Tablet or phoneFavouritesPhoto grid

    The supermarket

    Thousands of SKUs and a queue. Scanning is the main input, and when a barcode fails they need categories and a dense grid to find the item fast.

    Tablet, landscapeCategories and sortBarcode

    Merchant

    Owns the account. Sets taxes, discounts, payment options and staff permissions, and is the one who reads the end-of-day report.

    Employee

    Logs in from the same screen with a different toggle. Sees what the merchant allows and spends nearly all their time in Sale and Look Up.

    Design decisions

    Four calls, and what each one cost.

    Decision 1

    Design phone and tablet together, in both orientations.

    The quick route was a phone design stretched to fit. A stretched phone wastes the one thing a tablet has, which is room to keep the cart visible while you add to it.

    What I chose: a two-pane tablet layout with a fixed cart, and a phone layout with a Review Sale bar. Same components, different arrangement.

    The cost

    Two layouts to design and review for every flow.

    Decision 2

    Let the merchant choose category view or item view.

    I could not find one default that was right for both a boutique and a supermarket. Whichever I picked, one of them would scroll past things they never sell.

    What I chose: a setting for the inventory view, plus sort by A to Z, Z to A, newest and popular, and a list or grid switch on the screen itself.

    The cost

    One more preference to explain and maintain.

    Decision 3

    Make payment look like a register.

    A dropdown of payment methods is compact and slow. Under pressure, people pick by position and shape before they read.

    What I chose: seven tiles in two groups, card-based on top, cash-like below. The selected tile fills with the brand tint. Amount, discount and tips are restated above so nobody charges the wrong total.

    The cost

    A taller sheet, which is why it rises from the bottom on phones.

    Decision 4

    Ask for interest at sign-up. Sell at the sale.

    Sales wanted the Stellar payments pitch early. I argued for later, because a merchant who has not made a sale yet has no reason to care about card rates.

    What I chose: sign-up asks only for consent to be contacted, with Use Cash Only as an equal choice and a promise of contact within 48 hours. The prompt returns at the payment step for non-cash sales.

    The cost

    Fewer payment applications on day one, in exchange for more merchants who reach a first sale.

    Iterations

    The home screen took three rounds.

    Most screens settled quickly. Home did not, and it is the screen that matters most.

    Round 1

    One list for everything

    The first version put customer, cart, totals and Charge in a single column with a Sale and Menu toggle.

    What went wrong: it was a cart with nowhere to add from. Anything beyond five items pushed Charge off the screen.
    Round 2

    Split the adding from the cart

    Adding moved to its own area with three modes. Look Up gained favourites, full inventory, barcode scan and sort.

    What went wrong: on a phone the cart was now a tap away, and people lost track of what was in it.
    Round 3

    Keep the cart in sight

    The phone got a Review Sale bar with a running item count. Selected items highlight in place, so the list itself shows what is in the cart.

    What stayed: this is the version that was signed off for both phone and tablet.
    How it was built

    AI did the repetitive half. I kept the judgement.

    AI was part of the workflow from library to QA. I used it where a spec was explicit and the work was repetitive, and kept it away from decisions about a merchant’s counter.

    1. 1Brief to boardFigma

      Requirements tracked line by line with a status, in the design file itself.

    2. 2TokensFigma variables

      Colour, type and effects defined once and inherited by every app in the family.

    3. 3ScreensPhone and tablet

      Built from library parts. Signed-off pages kept apart from work in review.

    4. 4Design to codeFigma MCP and agent

      The agent reads real nodes and tokens, so handoff starts from the file and not a screenshot.

    5. 5QA walkBrowser agent

      An agent walks sign-up, sale, payment and end of day and flags drift for my review.

    What AI drafted

    Repeated states, component scaffolding, first-pass code from Figma nodes and edge-case checklists.

    What I decided

    The three modes, grid density, where payments are sold, and what a cashier should never read twice.

    Where it got it wrong

    In one tablet frame the quantity buttons rendered as the words MINUS and PLUS, because an icon font fell back to text. A person sees that instantly. An agent reading the frame literally would ship it. Icons are now library components, checked before handoff.

    The whole day, on one system.

    Outcome and reflection

    What shipped, and what I would do next.

    The signed-off design covers a merchant’s full day on phone and tablet: sign-up with lead capture, two-role login, a skippable welcome, four ways to ring up, seven payment types, receipts in four formats, searchable orders and an end-of-day report. The same library now carries the kiosk, event ticketing, inventory count and label printing apps.

    What worked

    Sorting the brief by outcome before designing. It turned a feature list into three problems, and gave me grounds to say no to things that served none of them.

    What I would change

    I would put the first build in front of working cashiers sooner. The Review Sale bar came out of round three and a morning at a real counter would have found it in round one.

    What comes next

    Automated token sync across the app family, offline behaviour for unreliable shop wifi, and measuring time from app open to Charge as the product’s one speed metric.
