The four principlesAnything that did not serve one of these did not get designed.

Show the number before asking for anything

The quote, rate, fee and arrival time are visible to someone who has not signed up and may never sign up.

Ask for the minimum, as late as the law allows

Verification runs in the background. The app stops you only at payment, and says why every field is required.

Catch failure before the money moves

Account, routing code and recipient name are checked before the user can continue.

Every state is a designed state

Pending, on hold, failed, rate expired, limit reached, offline, under review. If the system can be in it, there is a screen for it.

    The idea

    A cheaper rate is not the wedge. Being believed is.

    Senders stay with providers that cost them more because they trust them. So the product had to earn belief before it asked for anything.

    The client

    The licences, corridors and payout partners were in place. The product that could finish a first transfer was not.

    Context and constraints: a licensed business without a product that could finish the job.

    I joined to design the customer side end to end: the whole sender journey from splash screen to receipt, and every state that goes wrong in between. My job was not to remove the complexity. It was to make sure the user never pays for it in confusion.

    01 constraint

    Verification runs through a third party SDK

    Its interface and timing are fixed. I could design everything around it and nothing inside it.

    02 constraint

    Required fields are set by regulation

    A recipient in Nigeria needs different details from one in India. I could change order and framing, not the list.

    03 constraint

    Payout partners behave how they behave

    Some confirm in minutes, some in days, some reject silently. The design had to be honest about that.

    04 constraint

    One designer

    Which pushed everything toward a strict component system rather than hand-drawn screens.

    The problem

    Two identity walls, one cost claim nobody believes.

    A first-time sender has to pass KYC for themselves, then enter a stranger’s legal and bank details perfectly. All before they have any reason to trust the number on the screen.

    01

    Belief has to come before the signup wall

    If the first thing the app does is ask for a phone number, the user has nothing to evaluate and every reason to compare elsewhere.

    02

    Two identity walls, back to back

    KYC for the sender, then full legal details for a recipient. The second arrives when the user thinks they are nearly done.

    03

    The money disappears after you send it

    The sender gets a confirmation and silence. The recipient gets nothing. Both call support, and both tell the family it went wrong.

    Who it is for

    Three people use this product. Only two are customers.

    Built from corridor data, support themes and published field research on how migrant workers send money home.

    01Primary

    The monthly sender

    Works in the UK or US and sends to the same two or three people in India, Nigeria or the Philippines, usually within a day of payday. Checks three apps before committing.

    What they want
    The same transfer as last month, fast, at a rate they can verify.
    Designed for them
    Recently sent pinned on top, one tap repeat, saved payout methods, rates on the home screen.
    What loses them
    A flow that treats every send as a first send, and a rate that moves before paying.
    02Highest value, highest risk

    The first-timer under pressure

    Someone at home needs money now: a medical bill, school fees, an emergency. First money app, in a hurry, often in a second language, and they have heard the stories.

    What they want
    To know what it costs and that it will arrive, before committing to anything.
    Designed for them
    Full quote with no account, language on screen two, four named steps, non-blocking verification.
    What loses them
    Any wall before they know the cost. Every unexplained field. Any failure they cannot interpret.
    03Not a customer, still a user

    The recipient

    The person the money is for. No account, possibly no smartphone, often in another time zone. When they cannot see the transfer, they ask the sender, who contacts support.

    What they want
    A straight answer to one question: where is the money.
    Designed for them
    Public tracking by transaction ID with no login, shareable status, a quiet route to their own account.
    What loses them
    A tracking page behind a login, which is the category default.
    Research

    Everyone competes on the fee. I designed for the payout.

    Before designing anything, I ran one task through every competitor: send £1,000 from the UK to Nigeria, and note where each one asks for what.

    Key findings

    1. The cost is two numbers

      People see the fee, not the exchange rate margin. The margin is often the bigger half.

      Payout became the largest number on the screen.
    2. Trust travels through people

      New users rely on colleagues to get started and hesitate at unfamiliar apps.

      The recipient became a designed-for audience.
    3. Language is a constraint

      Lower digital literacy and non-Latin documents turn forms into rejections.

      Nine languages, chosen on screen two.
    4. Failure arrives late

      A wrong digit on Tuesday is a stuck transfer on Friday and a support call on Monday.

      Pre-flight validation on every bank detail.
    5. The second transfer is the business

      People send monthly, to the same two or three people.

      Repeat was designed before first-time send.

    Four inputs went in: competitor teardowns run as one timed task, published field research on migrant remittance behaviour, corridor rules and required fields from compliance, and support themes, read for what people complain about after the money leaves.

    Design goal

    Front-load honesty, strip the first transfer to the legal minimum, and never let a transfer fail quietly.

    Problem statement

    Three statements, three questions.

    I kept these on the wall for the whole project. Anything that did not serve one of them did not get designed.

    Problem 01

    A first-time sender comparing three apps has no reliable way to tell which is cheapest, because the real cost is split between a fee they can see and a margin they cannot.

    How might we

    Make the true cost checkable in under ten seconds, by someone who has not signed up and may never sign up?

    Answered in: pre-auth calculator, payout as hero number, openable fee, rate lock
    Problem 02

    A motivated user who wants to send money today is stopped twice by identity requirements, once for themselves and once for their recipient, and most do not come back.

    How might we

    Keep a user moving while verification runs, and ask for recipient details in a way that feels required rather than intrusive?

    Answered in: non-blocking KYC, banner states, four named steps, legal framing
    Problem 03

    Once the money leaves, neither the sender nor the recipient can see it, so every delay becomes a support call and a story about money going missing.

    How might we

    Give both sides of a transfer the same view of where the money is, without making the recipient sign up?

    Answered in: timestamped timeline, copyable references, public tracking, honest hold states

    How it was built

    One designer, a pipeline, and a review at every stage.

    Drawing 328 screens by hand and handing over a redline document was never going to fit. So I reviewed every stage of a pipeline instead of drawing every screen.

    1. 1Structure the file for a machine

      Named nodes, auto layout, one component per pattern, tokens as the source of truth.

    2. 2Batch the states, not the screens

      166 of the 328 are states. Built as variants, generated, then reviewed and corrected.

    3. 3Figma MCP into code

      Engineering received working markup on the same tokens, not a document to interpret.

    4. 4Agent-walked QA

      An agent walks the built flows and flags anything that drifts from the Figma frame.

    Human in the loop

    A messy file produces messy output, and no prompt fixes that.

    What I kept

    Every call on responsive behaviour, interaction states and content hierarchy. Reviewing and correcting each generated set of states.

    What the pipeline did

    The typing. It read real node data, dimensions, tokens and layer order rather than guessing from a screenshot, and it caught the dull inconsistencies that usually survive to release.

    Handoff and collaboration

    A 328 screen spec, four engineers, no meeting per screen.

    The work of handoff here is not exporting assets. It is making the conditional logic legible, because most of what makes this app complicated is invisible on any single screen.

    01

    The file is the flow document

    153 connections mapped, so an engineer can walk any journey, failure branches included, without asking what happens next.

    02

    States as variants, not screens

    Error, empty and loading live on the component, so it is obvious they are the same element in a different condition.

    03

    Conditionals called out

    Three things change what a user sees: verification state, corridor rules and limit tier. Each is annotated where it applies.

    04

    Compliance reviewed the copy

    Every line that states a legal reason, the consent wording and the sanctions hold message went through compliance first.

    05

    Weekly walkthrough

    One session a week with engineering and compliance, walking one flow end to end. It surfaced corridor rules I would not have known to ask about.

    06

    Tokens as the contract

    Colour, type and spacing as named tokens, so a change to primary is one edit rather than a search through 328 screens.

    Outcome

    A build-ready app, delivered as one specification.

    Every flow, every state and every error, with the design system and handoff behind it. A case study should not claim metrics it cannot show, so below are the four numbers we instrumented instead.

    Measure 01

    Quote to signup rate

    Does giving the full quote away convert better than gating it? The clearest counterfactual.

    Measure 02

    KYC completion

    The test of non-blocking verification plus banners. The industry loses 40 to 68 percent here.

    Measure 03

    Failed payouts per thousand

    Whether name and account validation earns its place in the flow.

    Measure 04

    Second transfer in 30 days

    Whether tracking, receipts and recently sent produce a repeat sender.

    What I would change

    Three things I would do next, and one I would argue for again anywhere.

    Shorten recipient creation

    Four steps is defensible, but address could be deferred on corridors where no payout partner demands it. I would go and get the per-corridor field data.

    Give cash pickup its own journey

    It ends at a physical counter with a person and an ID check. It deserves more than a shared template.

    Finish right to left

    Shipping a language you have not laid out properly is a promise you have not kept.
