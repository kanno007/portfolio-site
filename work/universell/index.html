Universell puts CRM, sales, inventory, payments, communication and marketing in a single subscription for small businesses in the United States. Library first

The component library came before the screens. Product files only use instances, so a fix to a table header lands in forty-five tools and on the POS at once.

Orange is a signal

The old UI painted every surface brand orange. Now it marks three things only: where you are, what to do next, and what needs attention.

Nobody is forced to switch

The new UI shipped behind a toggle with a way back on every screen. The POS welcome has Skip on every card. People choose their moment.

Sort the brief before drawing

Both briefs arrived as feature lists. Each was sorted into three problems first, which gave grounds to say no to anything that served none of them.

    Context

    A platform that had outgrown its own interface, and a till that had to fit three shops.

    The promise is simple to say. A shop owner pays one monthly price and stops juggling ten or twenty subscriptions. Customers, stock, invoices, phone calls and campaigns live in one place, and the data moves between them without anyone retyping it.

    The product already did most of this. What it lacked was a shared language. Each module had been built when it was needed, so each one behaved a little differently. A table in Inventory did not work like a table in CRM. New merchants signed up for a platform and met forty separate tools.

    The POS is the part of Universell that lives on the counter. It ships next to Stellar payments, so it has two jobs: take money quickly, and help a new merchant become a payments customer without feeling sold to. It had to work on a phone at a market stall and on a tablet at a supermarket lane.

    The platform

    CRM, marketing, inventory, analytics, communications, business operations and tickets, in one sidebar grouped by the job being done.

    The POS

    Sales, payments, inventory look-up, customers, staff and reporting in one app, with three modes on the home screen and seven payment types in one sheet.

    One system under both

    The same tokens, the same Noto Sans, the same rule that orange means act. The library also carries the kiosk, ticketing, inventory count and label printing apps.

    Two products, one platform

    The shop in the back office and the shop at the counter.

    A merchant who learns one Universell table has learned every table, including the orders list on the till. That was the point of doing both on one library.

    What they share

    The decisions that were made once and used twice.

    Most of what makes the platform and the till feel like one company was settled in the library before either had a finished screen.

    One library

    Styles, elements, actions, data entry, navigation, calendar, tables, alerts and assets, each on its own page with every state drawn out. The POS runs on the same library.

    Orange means act

    On the platform it marks where you are and what to do next. On the till it is reserved for actions and selection, so the thing to tap is the orange thing.

    Noto Sans, thirteen sizes

    Free, covers almost every script a customer name might use, and its numerals stay readable in dense tables and on a keypad.

    Status has its own colours

    Pending reads amber and Blocked reads red on the platform. Paid, refunded, open and failed each have a colour on the POS. Neither borrows the accent.

    Nobody is pushed

    The platform ships behind a Try the new UI switch with a way back. The POS asks only for consent to be contacted and puts Use Cash Only beside it as an equal choice.

    Leads flow back

    Every POS sign-up lands in Universell as a lead tagged for payments. Whatever a Catcher catches lands on the right record. Nothing is retyped.

    How it was built

    Audit, library, modules, then the counter.

    The platform came first and set the library. The POS was designed on that library a module later, with AI doing the repetitive half and a browser agent walking the flows.

    Who decided what

    What AI did

    Repeated states, component scaffolding, first-pass code from Figma nodes and edge-case checklists.

    What stayed with people

    The three problems in each brief, the navigation model, what orange is allowed to mean, the three POS modes, grid density, where payments are sold, and what a cashier should never read twice.

    Where it went wrong

    New structure, old paint

    The first library kept the orange surfaces and only fixed structure. Long forms on an orange card were hard to scan. It took three rounds to flip the ratio.

    A cart with nowhere to add from

    The first POS home put everything in one column. Anything beyond five items pushed Charge off the screen. Home took three rounds too.

    MINUS and PLUS

    In one tablet frame an icon font fell back to text. A person sees it instantly; an agent reading the frame literally would ship it. Icons are library components now.

    Outcome

    One product where there used to be many, and a till that belongs to it.

    The redesign runs across Activity, Marketing, Inventory, Analytics, Tickets, Communications, Business Operations and the console, all from one library. The POS covers a merchant’s full day on phone and tablet.

    What I would do next, across Universell

    01

    Name things once, early

    Messenger and Team Messaging are the same place. On a platform this wide, a shared glossary on day one is cheaper than a clean-up later.

    02

    Start with role-based tokens

    The palette began as named colours and grew roles afterwards. Dark theme, and the POS, would both have been faster if every colour had been defined by its job from the start.

    03

    Put the till in front of cashiers sooner

    The Review Sale bar came out of round three. A morning at a real counter would have found it in round one.
